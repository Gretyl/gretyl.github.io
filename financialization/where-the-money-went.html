Where the Money Went

The plumbing of shareholder primacy.

Forty‑two years after SEC Rule 10b‑18 created a safe harbor for stock buybacks, the cash flowing through the U.S. corporate sector has been re‑routed. The pipes that once fed productive capacity now feed share price — and the borrowed money on the inflow side is doing more of the work than at any point in the post‑war era.

An infographic in three panels  ·  The third
The Question

If the first panel showed corporations withdrawing equity from public markets, and the second showed wealth pooling at the top, the third must show the boardroom decision that connects them. The diagrams below trace one dollar of U.S. nonfinancial corporate cash flow through its competing uses in two reference years — 1982, the year of Rule 10b‑18, and 2024, the most recent full year on record. All figures are stated in real 2025 dollars to match Panel I.

Drawn at proportional scale to each other: the 2024 corporate sector handles roughly 3.8 times the inflation‑adjusted dollar volume of 1982. The morphology of the diagram is the story. The capex channel shrinks as a share of the whole. The shareholder‑return channels — dividends and, especially, buybacks — engorge. And a second inflow appears: borrowed money, large enough to fund most of what flows out to shareholders.

[02]   Three Structural Moments · Annotated

When the pipes were re‑soldered.

Each moment below is annotated identically on Panels I and II. The cash‑flow morphology shifts in lockstep with the equity‑market behavior and the wealth concentration.

1
1982 · Nov 17
Rule 10b‑18 adopted
The SEC creates a safe harbor for open‑market repurchases under §10(b) of the Exchange Act. The buyback channel becomes legally usable at scale. The 1982 Sankey above shows the morphology of the corporate sector at the moment of the change: capex dominant, dividends modest, buybacks functionally zero. Panel I's data point for 1982 is +$6.3B real — corporations were still issuing more equity than they retired.
2
2007
Real‑terms trough
Net equity retirement hits −$838 billion (real) — the deepest single year in the 51‑year series. Pre‑GFC, the second LBO mega‑wave converged with record buybacks and cash‑financed M&A. JPMorgan estimated that ~30% of buybacks were debt‑funded in this era. The 2007 cash‑flow morphology resembled today's: capex still dominant, but payouts rivaling it, with the leverage doing real work.
3
2017 →
TCJA & the new normal
The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (P.L. 115‑97) drops the corporate rate from 35% to 21%. S&P 500 buybacks hit $806B in 2018, then a new record of $942.5B in 2024. The 2024 Sankey above is the steady state of this regime — borrowing as a parallel inflow, a wide crimson river to shareholders. The 1% buyback excise tax (effective 2023) has not bent the curve.

[04]   What's in the Sankey, and What Isn't

Three questions worth asking.

The diagrams above make analytical choices that a careful reader would want defended.

Why split each inflow proportionally across all outflows — wasn't the borrowed money used specifically for buybacks?

Money is fungible. Z.1 doesn't track which dollar funded which use, and within a firm's treasury, sources and uses are pooled before allocation. The proportional split shown above is the neutral default — it shows each inflow's share of total funds and each outflow's share of total uses, without claiming a one‑to‑one routing.

The marginal view — that incremental borrowing funded incremental shareholder payouts — has empirical support. JPMorgan Chase estimated that ~30% of buybacks were funded by corporate bonds in 2016–17 (cited in Lazonick, HBR Jan 2020). Mason's 2015 Roosevelt paper documents a structural break in the post‑1985 correlation between borrowing and payouts. Both views are defensible; the proportional split shown above is the more conservative one.

How are operating cash flow and net new debt derived if they aren't direct F.103 line items?

Operating cash flow is approximated as total internal funds from F.103 = after‑tax profits + capital consumption adjustment − inventory valuation adjustment. Net new debt is the year‑on‑year change in total nonfinancial corporate credit market liabilities (BCNSDODNS, the L.103 stock series, differenced).

Both are derivable from Z.1 but not single published rows. The figures shown above balance the cash‑flow identity (sources = uses) to within 0.5%; that's the integrity check. Outflow figures — capex, dividends, net equity retirement — are taken directly from F.103.

Why 1982 and 2024 as the snapshot pair — and not some other pair?

1982 is the policy inflection: Rule 10b‑18 was adopted Nov 17 of that year. It is also the inflection year annotated on Panel I. 2024 is the most recent full calendar year with finalized Z.1 data at the time of writing.

Panel I's continuous time series demonstrates that these are samples of a continuous trend rather than a chosen extremity. The morphology shift is monotonic on a multi‑decade horizon, episodic on a yearly one, and qualitatively visible across any pair of pre‑1984 / post‑1990 reference years.
